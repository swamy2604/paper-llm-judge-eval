# Method (DRAFT)

## 3. Method

### 3.1 Overview

The study is a controlled comparison of automated and human judgement on
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) answers. One generator model produces
answers to 600 questions drawn from two public benchmarks. Three judge
models, each in two prompting modes, grade every answer. A human rater
grades the same answers on the same rubric. Agreement is measured with
Cohen's kappa and Spearman correlation, and systematic failure modes are
counted and illustrated.

### 3.2 Datasets and sampling

Two public question-answering benchmarks are used, both built from
Wikipedia:

- **HotpotQA** (Yang et al., 2018), distractor setting, train split
  (90,447 items). Each question requires reasoning over two or more
  supporting passages, and each item ships with relevant passages mixed
  with distractors, mirroring a real RAG retriever. 300 questions are
  sampled with seed 42.
- **Natural Questions** (Kwiatkowski et al., 2019), validation split
  (7,830 items). Each item is a real user query against a Wikipedia
  article, with a long answer (a passage) and a short answer (a span).
  The validation split is used instead of train because the train split
  (~87k items with multi-megabyte documents each) is impractical to
  stream for a 300-item sample on free compute; validation is the split
  the NQ paper itself evaluates on. Only items with a short answer (or a
  yes/no answer) are eligible, matching HotpotQA's answerable-only
  design. 300 questions are sampled with seed 42 (reservoir sampling
  over the streamed split).

For HotpotQA, the context is the item's own passages. For Natural
Questions, the context is the long-answer byte span from the document
text, plus a 500-byte window on each side, with HTML tags stripped and
whitespace collapsed. Document text is reconstructed exactly from the
token byte-offset table, without HTML parsing or a detokenizer.

The full sample (600 items) is stored as `data/full_sample.json` with a
fixed seed, so any researcher can reproduce the same sample. The pilot
study (Section 4) uses the first 50 HotpotQA items of the same design.

### 3.3 Answer generation

Answers are generated by **Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct**, loaded in 4-bit
(bitsandbytes) on a free Colab T4 GPU, temperature 0, maximum 120 new
tokens. The prompt is:

> Answer the question using ONLY the information in the passages below.
> If the passages do not contain the answer, say "I don't know".

The instruction to abstain is deliberate: a RAG system that says "I don't
know" when the sources are silent is behaving correctly, and the study
measures whether judges reward that behaviour. A single generator is used
so that differences in judge agreement cannot be confounded with
differences between generators; this is a limitation (Section 5.3).

Compute constraint: on a free T4 GPU (~15 GB), a very long context can
exhaust memory during attention. Passages are therefore capped at 8,000
characters (about 2,000 tokens); the number of truncated items is
counted and reported. If an item still runs out of memory, it is retried
once with a 3,000-character context and then recorded as skipped, never
silently filled in. Answers are written to disk one at a time as they
are generated, so an interrupted run loses at most one answer and can be
resumed.

### 3.4 LLM judges

Three judges from the same model family, at three sizes, so size is the
only variable that changes between them:

- Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct (small)
- Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (medium — the same model that generated the answers)
- Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct (large)

Each judge grades every answer in two modes, temperature 0, maximum 60
new tokens:

- **Direct:** a single overall score from 1 to 5, where 1 is completely
  wrong and 5 is fully correct and well supported. The prompt asks for
  only the number.
- **Rubric:** three separate scores from 1 to 5 — groundedness (every
  claim is supported by the passages), correctness (the answer matches
  the correct answer), completeness (the answer covers everything the
  question asks). The prompt requires the exact format
  `groundedness=X, correctness=Y, completeness=Z` and nothing else.

Judges always receive the passages, the question, and the answer. The
no-passages ablation (RQ2's third design choice) is not run in this
study; see Section 5.3.

Judge outputs are parsed defensively. The rubric parser first looks for
labelled scores, then falls back to three bare numbers in order
("5 5 4"), because the 7B judge frequently replies in that form (Section
4.3). Outputs that cannot be parsed into a number are excluded for that
dimension only, and the number of excluded judgements is reported.

### 3.5 Human ratings

The author rates every answer on the same three dimensions (groundedness,
correctness, completeness), each 1 to 5, with the gold answer and the
passages in front of them. A second rater independently rates a
100-answer subset, blind to the author's scores and to the gold answers
for groundedness, so inter-rater reliability can be reported.

Transparency note: for the pilot, the author asked the AI assistant to
draft the 50 ratings, and the draft is pending the author's review and
correction. Those ratings are therefore labelled AI-drafted throughout
this paper, and the pilot agreement numbers are reported as draft
results. The final paper will use ratings reviewed and corrected by the
author, with the AI-assisted drafting step disclosed in this section.

### 3.6 Agreement metrics

For each judge and each mode, agreement with the human rater is reported
as Cohen's kappa (agreement beyond chance, on the 1–5 scores) and
Spearman rank correlation. Direct-mode scores are compared against the
mean of the human rater's three dimension scores, rounded. Rubric-mode
scores are compared dimension by dimension. All six judge-by-mode
combinations are reported, whatever the numbers are.

### 3.7 Failure-mode analysis (RQ3)

Three systematic failure modes are measured:

- **Length bias:** the correlation between answer length (in words) and
  the judge-minus-human score gap. A positive correlation means the
  judge rewards long answers more than the human does.
- **Self-preference bias:** whether judges score answers from their own
  model family higher. Because all three judges are Qwen models and the
  generator is also a Qwen model, this test is confounded in this study:
  the 7B judge grading 7B-generated answers is the same-model case, and
  the result must be read as an upper bound on self-preference, not a
  clean measurement. A cross-family generator is future work.
- **Missed ungrounded claims:** the fraction of answers the human rater
  scored groundedness ≤ 2 that the judge scored ≥ 4 on groundedness
  (rubric) or overall (direct). Each case is inspected by hand and
  concrete examples are reported.

### 3.8 Reproducibility

All code, the sampling seed (42), the prompts, and the pilot data are
public at https://github.com/swamy2604/paper-llm-judge-eval. No paid
APIs or proprietary models are used: every model is an open-weight Qwen
model run on a free Colab T4 GPU, and every dataset is public. The
study uses no proprietary or employer data.
